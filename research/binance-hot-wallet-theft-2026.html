Binance's $6.3B Theft: Nobody Stole a Key

On 29 October 2026 about $6.3 billion left Binance's hot wallets on ten blockchains in roughly half an hour. It is the largest theft on record, about four times the Bybit heist of 2025. Binance says it has found no evidence that a wallet private key leaked. That one sentence is the whole lesson: the money moved because Binance's own systems were made to sign for it.

Crypto Theft EIP-7702 Signing Infrastructure North Korea

TL;DR for defenders. Known as of 1 November 2026; Binance has not published its post-mortem yet. Around 20:05 UTC on 29 October, roughly $6.3-6.4 billion left Binance hot wallets on ten chains. The FBI attributed it to North Korea's TraderTraitor on 31 October. Binance says the incident may have started with a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop that gave the attacker a service container, that it later found additional points of breach, and that it has no evidence of a private key leak. On Ethereum the attacker used 50 EIP-7702 authorisations, each validly signed with the hot wallet's own key, to make the wallet run an attacker contract. If you run a hot wallet: alert the moment one of your wallets has delegation code (a script for that is below), make your signer refuse EIP-7702 authorisations and anything else it was not built to sign, and block the attacker addresses that TRM and Elliptic have labelled.

Nobody stole a key

Exchanges spend a great deal on keeping private keys away from people: hardware security modules, multi-party computation, key shards in different places. All of that answers one question, "can someone take our key?" On 29 October the answer, as far as Binance can tell, was still no. The money left anyway.

Every outgoing transaction on every chain carried a valid signature from a Binance wallet. Nothing was forged. Blockchain analytics firm TRM Labs puts it plainly: the most likely explanation is that the attacker could make Binance's signing system sign transactions of its choosing. That moves the defender's question from "is the key safe" to "what can make our signer sign".

It is not new. Chainalysis watched the Binance funds mix with those from the Bitget hot wallet theft five weeks earlier, where, TRM reports, the exchange's own withdrawal controls approved the transfers. The FBI tied the same group to Bybit in February 2025, where the signers' own web interface was swapped. Different exchanges, one playbook: reach the machinery that approves money, not the key behind it.

How a hot wallet ends up running someone else's code

Most of the chains were drained with ordinary transfers: the hot wallet signed a payment to the attacker, and the chain accepted it because the signature was valid. Ethereum is the interesting one, because there the attacker did something that outlived a single transaction.

EIP-7702, live on Ethereum since the Pectra upgrade in May 2025, lets a plain wallet (an "externally owned account") hand its behaviour to a smart contract. It works like this:

  1. The wallet's key signs a small authorisation: "run the code at address X", for a given chain and a given wallet nonce (its transaction count).
  2. Anyone can put that authorisation into a type 4 transaction and send it. The wallet's owner does not have to be the one who submits it.
  3. When it lands, the wallet's code becomes a 23-byte delegation indicator, 0xef0100 followed by address X. From then on, every call to the wallet runs X's code with the wallet's balance and authority.
  4. The delegation stays until the key signs a new authorisation that replaces or clears it.

The attacker's X was a sweeper contract that accepted instructions only from the attacker's operator wallet. Once it was attached to Binance 14, emptying the wallet needed no further signature from Binance. That is why the sweeps kept coming for more than three hours, catching every new deposit, until Binance revoked the delegation.

Why 50 authorisations? An authorisation only works if its nonce equals the wallet's current transaction count. A busy exchange hot wallet's count moves constantly. TRM says the attacker signed authorisations for 50 consecutive nonces in advance, so that one of them was certain to match at the moment the transaction landed. TRM's advice on that shape is worth repeating word for word: a request for many authorisations across a range of nonces "should be treated as an indicator of compromise".

Here is the part that matters for defenders. Each of those 50 authorisations had to be signed with Binance 14's own key. If, as Binance says, the key never leaked, then Binance's signing system signed them. Elliptic reports Teng saying the EIP-7702 use "did not pass through our normal approval controls". That is our inference from public facts, not something Binance has stated, but it points at the control that failed: not the key's storage, but what the signer was willing to sign.

The path Binance has described

Binance's public account so far is short, and it has already grown once:

  • The incident "may involve a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop, which allowed an attacker to gain access to a service container" (Binance security update, 30 October).
  • From there the attacker "submitted unauthorized withdrawal transactions". Teng added that the attacker "did not simply use one engineer's permissions" and that it was not "a single 'one-click' action".
  • Hours later Binance said it had "uncovered additional points of breach" and called it "a complex attack".

Read together: an engineer's machine led to a container inside the platform, and something reachable from that container could get withdrawals and authorisations signed across ten chains. What that container ran, how it reached the signer, and what the other breach points were, Binance has not said. We mark everything past that as unknown.

The entry point fits the group's record. In the Bybit case, Mandiant traced the first step to a developer at the wallet supplier Safe running a fake stock-trading Docker project, followed by the theft of that developer's cloud session. An engineer's laptop is the cheapest door into production that exists, and TraderTraitor keeps walking through it.

What a defender can watch

Four things, in order of how directly they would have mattered here. The first two are for anyone who holds a hot wallet on an Ethereum-compatible chain. The last two depend on your platform looking something like what Binance described.

1. Your own wallets gaining delegation code

A hot wallet that never delegates should never have code. If it suddenly does, something signed an EIP-7702 authorisation for it, and the wallet may already be running someone else's contract. This is cheap to check: one eth_getCode call per address against your own node. An empty answer (0x) is a plain wallet; an answer that starts 0xef0100 is a delegation, and the rest of it is the contract's address. TRM gives the same advice: treat any delegation on a treasury wallet as a trigger for an immediate alert. 

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Alert if any wallet you list has EIP-7702 delegation code.

Read-only: one eth_getCode call per address against your own node.
A plain wallet (EOA) has no code. A delegated one has the 23-byte
indicator 0xef0100 followed by the 20-byte address it now runs.

usage: RPC_URL=https://your-node python3 check_7702.py wallets.txt
exit:  0 all clean, 2 delegation found, 1 error
"""
import json
import os
import sys
import urllib.request

PREFIX = "0xef0100"


def get_code(rpc, address):
    body = json.dumps({"jsonrpc": "2.0", "id": 1, "method": "eth_getCode",
                       "params": [address, "latest"]}).encode()
    req = urllib.request.Request(rpc, body, {"Content-Type": "application/json",
                                             "User-Agent": "check_7702/1"})
    with urllib.request.urlopen(req, timeout=10) as r:
        reply = json.load(r)
    if "error" in reply:
        raise RuntimeError(reply["error"])
    return reply["result"].lower()


def classify(code):
    if code in ("0x", "0x0", ""):
        return "clean", None
    if code.startswith(PREFIX) and len(code) == 2 + 46:
        return "DELEGATED", "0x" + code[len(PREFIX):]
    return "has-code", None  # a contract, not a plain wallet


def main():
    rpc = os.environ.get("RPC_URL")
    if not rpc or len(sys.argv) != 2:
        sys.exit(__doc__)
    found = False
    for line in open(sys.argv[1]):
        addr = line.split("#")[0].strip()
        if not addr:
            continue
        state, target = classify(get_code(rpc, addr))
        print(f"{addr} {state}" + (f" -> {target}" if target else ""))
        found = found or state == "DELEGATED"
    sys.exit(2 if found else 0)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    try:
        main()
    except (OSError, RuntimeError, ValueError) as e:
        sys.exit(f"error: {e}")

Put one address per line in wallets.txt (a # starts a comment), run it every block or every minute from cron or your monitoring system, and page on exit code 2. It uses only the Python standard library. We ran it on 1 November against a public Ethereum node: a plain wallet came back clean, a token contract has-code, and three wallets taken from recent type 4 transactions DELEGATED, each with the address it now runs. Check every chain where you hold a hot wallet and EIP-7702 is live, not only Ethereum mainnet.

Be honest about what this buys. It fires after the delegation lands. At Binance the first sweep came about three and a half minutes later, so a one-minute check would not have saved the first sweep. It would have cut short the three hours of repeat sweeps, and it turns "we noticed when the money was gone" into a page with the attacker's contract address in it. Have a pre-approved way to sign a revocation ready, as TRM recommends, or the alert just tells you how fast you are losing.

2. A signer that refuses what it was not built to sign

This is the control, not a query. An exchange hot wallet signs a small set of things: transfers to approved destinations, in amounts inside its limits, of transaction types its runbook lists. Everything else should be refused before any key is touched:

  • EIP-7702 authorisations. An authorisation is signed over the byte 0x05 followed by the chain, the contract address and a nonce. A hot wallet that has no reason to delegate should never sign one. Refuse type 4 transactions too.
  • Batches. Many authorisations, or many signatures across a range of nonces, in one request or one short window. TRM calls this an indicator of compromise; treat it as one and stop.
  • Raw digests. A signing endpoint that will sign any 32-byte hash it is handed cannot tell a payment from an authorisation, because the hash hides what it is. Policy has to sit where the request is still readable, before it is hashed. If your signer only ever sees hashes, the policy is not in the signer at all.

Teng's own description, a delegation that bypassed normal approval controls, is exactly the gap this closes. Elliptic is our source for that quote and Binance has not published it in writing, so we treat it as reported, not confirmed.

3. A person reaching into the signing workload

Binance's path went from a laptop to a service container. If your withdrawal or signing services run on Kubernetes, the API server's audit log records every time a person opens a shell in a pod, attaches to it, port-forwards to it or adds a debug container. In the namespace that holds the signer, that should almost never happen outside an approved change. SigmaHQ already has a generic rule for exec into a container; this version is scoped to signing workloads, adds the other three ways in, and leaves out service accounts: 

title: Interactive Access Into a Withdrawal or Signing Workload
id: d4174fd7-7ad4-47ab-93be-f76e57f669b2
status: experimental
description: >
    A person opened a shell, attached, port-forwarded or added a debug
    container in the namespace that runs your withdrawal or signing service.
    Binance said its October 2026 theft may have started with a DevOps
    engineer's laptop that gave the attacker a service container. Put your
    own signing namespace in selection_ns; service accounts are excluded.
references:
    - https://umbrasec.dev/research/binance-hot-wallet-theft-2026
author: UMBRASEC
date: 2026-11-01
tags:
    - attack.execution
    - attack.t1609
logsource:
    product: kubernetes
    service: audit
detection:
    selection_ns:
        objectRef.namespace: 'EDIT-ME-signing-namespace'
        objectRef.resource: 'pods'
    selection_exec:
        verb: 'create'
        objectRef.subresource:
            - 'exec'
            - 'attach'
            - 'portforward'
    selection_debug:
        verb: 'patch'
        objectRef.subresource: 'ephemeralcontainers'
    filter_service_accounts:
        user.username|startswith: 'system:serviceaccount:'
    condition: selection_ns and (selection_exec or selection_debug) and not filter_service_accounts
falsepositives:
    - An on-call engineer debugging the signing service during an approved change
level: high

Replace EDIT-ME-signing-namespace with your own namespace (a list works). Until you do, it matches nothing. It is an article snippet, parsed with pySigma but not in our sigma pack: we will decide on pack rules once Binance's post-mortem says what its platform actually was.

4. An engineer's laptop reaching production

The first step in this attack, and in Bybit's, was an engineer's machine. The useful question is not "was a laptop infected" but "what can a laptop reach". Hunt for production credentials, kubeconfigs and cloud sessions used from engineer endpoints outside change windows, and for a session that keeps the engineer's working hours but not their usual places. Mandiant saw exactly that pattern at Safe: the attacker kept to the developer's schedule so the stolen session stayed live. What to do this week

  1. Check every hot wallet for delegation code now, with the script above or your own node, on every EIP-7702 chain you hold funds on. Then schedule it.
  2. Ask your signing team one question: would our signer sign an EIP-7702 authorisation, or a batch of them across 50 nonces, if a service asked it to? If the answer is "yes" or "not sure", that is the finding.
  3. Block the attacker addresses that TRM and Elliptic have labelled, and screen deposits against them. Check the FBI alert for its own list.
  4. Keep a revocation ready. A pre-approved, tested way to sign a clearing authorisation (address zero) and to pause withdrawals. Binance's outflows stopped when it paused withdrawals.
  5. Separate the paths. TRM recommends separating signing infrastructure and approval paths by chain and wallet tier, so one breach does not sign for ten chains at once.
  6. Map what an engineer's laptop can reach in your signing and withdrawal systems, and alert on interactive access to those workloads.

Honest limitations

This is written two to three days after the theft and before Binance's post-mortem. The laptop and service container are Binance's preliminary account, and Binance has since said there were additional points of breach it has not described. How the attacker got from a container to signed withdrawals is not public; where we say Binance's own signer signed, that is our inference from Binance's statement that no key leaked. Teng's quote about approval controls reaches us through Elliptic, not a Binance document. Loss figures differ between Binance, TRM and Elliptic ($6.3 to $6.43 billion) because of pricing and counting choices. We could not read the FBI alert, Hacken's analysis or WIRED's reporting directly and cite none of them for detail. The delegation check ran against live chain data, but it only sees a delegation after it lands. The Kubernetes rule is a pySigma-parsed snippet, not tested against real audit logs, and it only applies if your signing path runs on Kubernetes. We do not know that Binance's does. We will update this article when the post-mortem is published.
