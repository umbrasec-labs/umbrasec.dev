Binance's $6.3B Theft: Nobody Stole a Key

On 29 October 2026 about $6.3 billion left Binance's hot wallets on ten blockchains in roughly half an hour. It is the largest theft on record, about four times the Bybit heist of 2025. Binance says it has found no evidence that a wallet private key leaked. That one sentence is the whole lesson: the money moved because Binance's own systems were made to sign for it.

Updated 2026-11-04: Binance published its initial findings on 3 November. The attack began months earlier with engineers' machines: a tampered Homebrew install in June and a malicious coding assignment from a recruitment approach in September. An internal AI tool was then used to reach two more engineers, and a container deployment token led to Binance's wallet signing service. The timeline, the path, the Kubernetes rule, the ATT&CK table, What to do this week and Honest limitations now reflect that. One correction: pausing withdrawals did not stop the Ethereum sweeps.

Crypto Theft EIP-7702 Signing Infrastructure North Korea

TL;DR for defenders. Known as of 4 November 2026, after Binance's initial findings of 3 November (a full report is still to come). Around 20:05 UTC on 29 October, roughly $6.3-6.4 billion left Binance hot wallets on ten chains. The FBI attributed it to North Korea's TraderTraitor on 31 October. Binance describes a months-long campaign against its wallet engineers: a malicious coding assignment from a recruitment approach infected one engineer's work computer, an internal AI tool was used from it to take over two more, and a container deployment token found on one of them let the attacker deploy a container that reached the internal wallet signing service. No private key leaked. On Ethereum the attacker used 50 EIP-7702 authorisations, each validly signed with the hot wallet's own key, to make the wallet run an attacker contract, and pausing withdrawals did not stop it. If you run a hot wallet: alert the moment one of your wallets has delegation code (a script for that is below), make your signer refuse EIP-7702 authorisations and anything else it was not built to sign, keep a tested revocation ready, and keep take-home coding tasks off the machines that can reach your signer.

Nobody stole a key

Exchanges spend a great deal on keeping private keys away from people: hardware security modules, multi-party computation, key shards in different places. All of that answers one question, "can someone take our key?" On 29 October the answer, as far as Binance can tell, was still no. The money left anyway.

Every outgoing transaction on every chain carried a valid signature from a Binance wallet. Nothing was forged. Blockchain analytics firm TRM Labs puts it plainly: the most likely explanation is that the attacker could make Binance's signing system sign transactions of its choosing. That moves the defender's question from "is the key safe" to "what can make our signer sign".

It is not new. Chainalysis watched the Binance funds mix with those from the Bitget hot wallet theft five weeks earlier, where, TRM reports, the exchange's own withdrawal controls approved the transfers. The FBI tied the same group to Bybit in February 2025, where the signers' own web interface was swapped. Different exchanges, one playbook: reach the machinery that approves money, not the key behind it.

How a hot wallet ends up running someone else's code

Most of the chains were drained with ordinary transfers: the hot wallet signed a payment to the attacker, and the chain accepted it because the signature was valid. Ethereum is the interesting one, because there the attacker did something that outlived a single transaction.

EIP-7702, live on Ethereum since the Pectra upgrade in May 2025, lets a plain wallet (an "externally owned account") hand its behaviour to a smart contract. It works like this:

  1. The wallet's key signs a small authorisation: "run the code at address X", for a given chain and a given wallet nonce (its transaction count).
  2. Anyone can put that authorisation into a type 4 transaction and send it. The wallet's owner does not have to be the one who submits it.
  3. When it lands, the wallet's code becomes a 23-byte delegation indicator, 0xef0100 followed by address X. From then on, every call to the wallet runs X's code with the wallet's balance and authority.
  4. The delegation stays until the key signs a new authorisation that replaces or clears it.

The attacker's X was a sweeper contract that accepted instructions only from the attacker's operator wallet. Once it was attached to Binance 14, emptying the wallet needed no further signature from Binance. That is why the sweeps kept coming for more than three hours, catching every new deposit, until Binance revoked the delegation.

Why 50 authorisations? An authorisation only works if its nonce equals the wallet's current transaction count. A busy exchange hot wallet's count moves constantly. TRM says the attacker signed authorisations for 50 consecutive nonces in advance, so that one of them was certain to match at the moment the transaction landed. TRM's advice on that shape is worth repeating word for word: a request for many authorisations across a range of nonces "should be treated as an indicator of compromise".

Here is the part that matters for defenders. Each of those 50 authorisations had to be signed with Binance 14's own key. If, as Binance says, the key never leaked, then Binance's signing system signed them. Elliptic reports Teng saying the EIP-7702 use "did not pass through our normal approval controls". When this article first ran, that was our inference. Binance's initial findings now say it outright, as the press reports them: the attacker reached the internal wallet signing service and used it to start transactions outside the normal withdrawal process, including the EIP-7702 permissions. The control that failed was not the key's storage, but what the signer was willing to sign and who could ask it.

The delegation also explains why pausing withdrawals was not enough. The authorisation already sat on-chain, outside Binance's withdrawal system, so the sweeper kept working until a new authorisation cleared it. Binance's wallets use multi-party computation, so no single key could sign that clearing authorisation on the spot; Binance had to build a procedure for it, and the delegation stayed until 00:10 UTC on 30 October, about four hours after it landed.

The path Binance has described

On 30 October Binance said the incident "may involve a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop, which allowed an attacker to gain access to a service container", and hours later that it had "uncovered additional points of breach". Its initial findings of 3 November fill in most of that. We have them from press reports (The Block, Security Affairs, crypto.news), which agree with each other; Binance's own text is an article on X that we could not open. As reported, the path ran in five steps:

  1. Reconnaissance through a failed first attempt (June). A wallet DevOps engineer ("Engineer A") installed a tampered Homebrew package while trying to install an AI coding tool. Binance caught it, replaced the device and rotated credentials. The attacker still came away knowing who worked on wallet infrastructure.
  2. A coding assignment from a job approach (September). Another engineer ("Engineer B") was approached through a legitimate recruitment platform by someone posing as a project director, had a video interview with a supposed HR representative and a second, in a chat app, with the supposed CEO of a company that does not exist, and was then told to download an assignment repository onto his work computer. Its setup code replaced pyenv, a Python version manager, with a malicious version that hid its activity and talked to an attacker server. Binance's endpoint tools did not detect it.
  3. An internal AI tool used to move sideways (20 October). From Engineer B's computer the attacker used an internal AI tool to take control of Engineer A's computer and that of a third engineer. Binance says it found no vulnerability in the AI model itself: the attacker went through an internal application with the compromised user's permissions. What the tool is, and what it was allowed to do on other people's machines, Binance has not said.
  4. A container deployment token on a laptop. Engineer A's computer held a token that could deploy containers.
  5. A container next to the signer (29 October). With the stolen credentials the attacker deployed a container and ran a script in it that reached the internal wallet signing service. From there it signed transfers on ten chains and the EIP-7702 authorisations on Ethereum.

Binance says it has closed the entry points, rebuilt the affected wallet environment and the internal AI tool, replaced the compromised devices, revoked the credentials and tokens involved, and engaged independent firms to review the incident. It is holding back some technical detail while the investigation continues. Still unknown: how the June compromise connects to September, what the AI tool could do, what the script did, and why the signing service accepted requests from a freshly deployed container.

The entry point fits the group's record. In the Bybit case, Mandiant traced the first step to a developer at the wallet supplier Safe running a fake stock-trading Docker project, followed by the theft of that developer's cloud session. Fake job interviews with a coding test are a long-running North Korean pattern. An engineer's laptop is the cheapest door into production that exists, and TraderTraitor keeps walking through it. What is new here is the middle step: an internal AI tool that could act on other engineers' machines became the lateral movement path.

What a defender can watch

Four things, in order of how directly they would have mattered here. The first two are for anyone who holds a hot wallet on an Ethereum-compatible chain. The last two depend on your platform looking something like what Binance described.

1. Your own wallets gaining delegation code

A hot wallet that never delegates should never have code. If it suddenly does, something signed an EIP-7702 authorisation for it, and the wallet may already be running someone else's contract. This is cheap to check: one eth_getCode call per address against your own node. An empty answer (0x) is a plain wallet; an answer that starts 0xef0100 is a delegation, and the rest of it is the contract's address. TRM gives the same advice: treat any delegation on a treasury wallet as a trigger for an immediate alert. 

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Alert if any wallet you list has EIP-7702 delegation code.

Read-only: one eth_getCode call per address against your own node.
A plain wallet (EOA) has no code. A delegated one has the 23-byte
indicator 0xef0100 followed by the 20-byte address it now runs.

usage: RPC_URL=https://your-node python3 check_7702.py wallets.txt
exit:  0 all clean, 2 delegation found, 1 error
"""
import json
import os
import sys
import urllib.request

PREFIX = "0xef0100"


def get_code(rpc, address):
    body = json.dumps({"jsonrpc": "2.0", "id": 1, "method": "eth_getCode",
                       "params": [address, "latest"]}).encode()
    req = urllib.request.Request(rpc, body, {"Content-Type": "application/json",
                                             "User-Agent": "check_7702/1"})
    with urllib.request.urlopen(req, timeout=10) as r:
        reply = json.load(r)
    if "error" in reply:
        raise RuntimeError(reply["error"])
    return reply["result"].lower()


def classify(code):
    if code in ("0x", "0x0", ""):
        return "clean", None
    if code.startswith(PREFIX) and len(code) == 2 + 46:
        return "DELEGATED", "0x" + code[len(PREFIX):]
    return "has-code", None  # a contract, not a plain wallet


def main():
    rpc = os.environ.get("RPC_URL")
    if not rpc or len(sys.argv) != 2:
        sys.exit(__doc__)
    found = False
    for line in open(sys.argv[1]):
        addr = line.split("#")[0].strip()
        if not addr:
            continue
        state, target = classify(get_code(rpc, addr))
        print(f"{addr} {state}" + (f" -> {target}" if target else ""))
        found = found or state == "DELEGATED"
    sys.exit(2 if found else 0)


if __name__ == "__main__":
    try:
        main()
    except (OSError, RuntimeError, ValueError) as e:
        sys.exit(f"error: {e}")

Put one address per line in wallets.txt (a # starts a comment), run it every block or every minute from cron or your monitoring system, and page on exit code 2. It uses only the Python standard library. We ran it on 1 November against a public Ethereum node: a plain wallet came back clean, a token contract has-code, and three wallets taken from recent type 4 transactions DELEGATED, each with the address it now runs. Check every chain where you hold a hot wallet and EIP-7702 is live, not only Ethereum mainnet.

Be honest about what this buys. It fires after the delegation lands. At Binance the first sweep came about three and a half minutes later, so a one-minute check would not have saved the first sweep. It would have cut short the three hours of repeat sweeps, and it turns "we noticed when the money was gone" into a page with the attacker's contract address in it. Have a pre-approved way to sign a revocation ready, as TRM recommends, or the alert just tells you how fast you are losing.

2. A signer that refuses what it was not built to sign

This is the control, not a query. An exchange hot wallet signs a small set of things: transfers to approved destinations, in amounts inside its limits, of transaction types its runbook lists. Everything else should be refused before any key is touched:

  • EIP-7702 authorisations. An authorisation is signed over the byte 0x05 followed by the chain, the contract address and a nonce. A hot wallet that has no reason to delegate should never sign one. Refuse type 4 transactions too.
  • Batches. Many authorisations, or many signatures across a range of nonces, in one request or one short window. TRM calls this an indicator of compromise; treat it as one and stop.
  • Raw digests. A signing endpoint that will sign any 32-byte hash it is handed cannot tell a payment from an authorisation, because the hash hides what it is. Policy has to sit where the request is still readable, before it is hashed. If your signer only ever sees hashes, the policy is not in the signer at all.

Teng's own description, a delegation that bypassed normal approval controls, is exactly the gap this closes. Elliptic is our source for that quote and Binance has not published it in writing, so we treat it as reported, not confirmed.

3. A person or a stolen token reaching into the signing workload

Binance's path ended with a deployed container next to its signing service, started with a deployment token taken from an engineer's computer. If your withdrawal or signing services run on Kubernetes, the API server's audit log records both kinds of reach: a person opening a shell in a pod, attaching to it, port-forwarding to it or adding a debug container, and any identity creating or changing a workload. In the namespace that holds the signer, the first should almost never happen outside an approved change, and the second should only ever come from your deployment pipeline. SigmaHQ already has a generic rule for exec into a container; this version is scoped to signing workloads, adds the other ways in, and adds workload changes by anyone other than your named deploy identity: 

title: Interactive Access or Unexpected Deployment in a Signing Namespace
id: d4174fd7-7ad4-47ab-93be-f76e57f669b2
status: experimental
description: >
    In the namespace that runs your withdrawal or signing service, a person
    opened a shell, attached, port-forwarded or added a debug container, or
    an identity other than your deploy pipeline created or changed a
    workload. Binance's October 2026 theft ended with a container deployed
    with a token taken from an engineer's computer, next to its wallet
    signing service. Put your signing namespace in selection_ns and your
    pipeline's identity in filter_deployer.
references:
    - https://umbrasec.dev/research/binance-hot-wallet-theft-2026
author: UMBRASEC
date: 2026-11-01
modified: 2026-11-04
tags:
    - attack.execution
    - attack.t1609
    - attack.t1610
logsource:
    product: kubernetes
    service: audit
detection:
    selection_ns:
        objectRef.namespace: 'EDIT-ME-signing-namespace'
    selection_exec:
        objectRef.resource: 'pods'
        verb: 'create'
        objectRef.subresource:
            - 'exec'
            - 'attach'
            - 'portforward'
    selection_debug:
        objectRef.resource: 'pods'
        verb: 'patch'
        objectRef.subresource: 'ephemeralcontainers'
    filter_service_accounts:
        user.username|startswith: 'system:serviceaccount:'
    selection_workload:
        verb:
            - 'create'
            - 'update'
            - 'patch'
        objectRef.resource:
            - 'pods'
            - 'deployments'
            - 'replicasets'
            - 'statefulsets'
            - 'daemonsets'
            - 'jobs'
            - 'cronjobs'
        objectRef.subresource: null
    filter_deployer:
        user.username: 'EDIT-ME-deploy-identity'
    filter_control_plane:
        user.username|startswith:
            - 'system:serviceaccount:kube-system:'
            - 'system:node:'
            - 'system:kube-'
    condition: selection_ns and (((selection_exec or selection_debug) and not filter_service_accounts) or (selection_workload and not filter_deployer and not filter_control_plane))
falsepositives:
    - An on-call engineer debugging the signing service during an approved change
    - A second deploy tool or operator that changes workloads in the namespace; add its identity to filter_deployer
level: high

Replace EDIT-ME-signing-namespace with your own namespace (a list works). Until you do, it matches nothing. Then put your pipeline's user name in filter_deployer before you turn it on, or every normal release will fire it. The control-plane filter keeps out the controllers that create pods and replica sets on a deployment's behalf, and the kubelet. The workload part only looks at the main object, so status updates and scaling do not count.

One blind spot matters here: if the stolen token is your deploy pipeline's own, this rule sees a normal deployment. Binance has not said whose token it was. Check where that identity's requests come from, too: a deployment from an engineer's address instead of your CI runners is the same event seen from the network side (sourceIPs in the same audit record). It is an article snippet, parsed with pySigma but not in our sigma pack; we have not tested it against real audit logs. Updated 2026-11-04: the first version covered only interactive access; deployments by anyone other than your pipeline were added after Binance's initial findings.

4. An engineer's laptop reaching production

The first step in this attack, and in Bybit's, was an engineer's machine. The useful question is not "was a laptop infected" but "what can a laptop reach". At Binance the answer was a container deployment token, sitting on a laptop, that could put code next to the signer. Hunt for production credentials, deploy tokens, kubeconfigs and cloud sessions used from engineer endpoints outside change windows, and for a session that keeps the engineer's working hours but not their usual places. Mandiant saw exactly that pattern at Safe: the attacker kept to the developer's schedule so the stolen session stayed live.

Both of Binance's ways in were developer tooling. Three things are worth a look on the machines of anyone who works on wallets or signing:

  • Outside code run on work machines. A take-home assignment or an "interview project" should never be cloned and set up on a machine that holds production access. If your EDR can see it, look for repositories cloned into download or temp folders whose setup step (an install script, npm install, pip install, make) starts a shell or a network connection.
  • Tool managers changed by something else. The September malware replaced pyenv. Its shims live in the user's ~/.pyenv folder and run every time the engineer types python, which makes them a quiet place to persist. A file write to ~/.pyenv/shims or ~/.pyenv/libexec by anything other than pyenv itself, or by a process running from a freshly cloned repository, is worth an alert. The same goes for Homebrew installed from anywhere but brew.sh.
  • Internal AI tools that can act on other people's machines. Binance's lateral movement went through an internal AI tool with the compromised user's permissions. If one of your tools can run commands or change files on a colleague's device, it is a remote administration tool. Give it an inventory entry, a log of every action and who asked for it, and alert when one user's requests start touching machines they have never touched before.

These are hunting leads, not tested rules: Binance has not published the indicators, and we have not run them in a lab. Our hijacked AI coding agents article covers the same asset, an engineer's access, from another angle.

What to do this week

  1. Check every hot wallet for delegation code now, with the script above or your own node, on every EIP-7702 chain you hold funds on. Then schedule it.
  2. Ask your signing team one question: would our signer sign an EIP-7702 authorisation, or a batch of them across 50 nonces, if a service asked it to? If the answer is "yes" or "not sure", that is the finding.
  3. Block the attacker addresses that TRM and Elliptic have labelled, and screen deposits against them. Check the FBI alert for its own list.
  4. Keep a revocation ready. A pre-approved, tested way to sign a clearing authorisation (address zero), and a way to pause withdrawals. Pausing withdrawals is not enough on its own: at Binance the delegation was already on-chain and kept sweeping until it was cleared, and an MPC wallet needed a special procedure to sign the clearing authorisation. Rehearse that procedure for your own wallets before you need it.
  5. Separate the paths. TRM recommends separating signing infrastructure and approval paths by chain and wallet tier, so one breach does not sign for ten chains at once.
  6. Map what an engineer's laptop can reach in your signing and withdrawal systems. Move deploy tokens off laptops, and alert on interactive access to those workloads and on deployments that do not come from your pipeline.
  7. Tell your wallet and platform engineers that interview assignments and other outside code never run on work machines, and give them somewhere else to run them.
  8. List your internal AI tools that can act on other people's machines, and check that each logs what it did and on whose request.

Honest limitations

This was first written two to three days after the theft and updated on 4 November after Binance's initial findings. Those findings are preliminary; Binance is holding back some technical detail and has independent firms reviewing the incident. Binance published them as an article on X that we could not open, so everything we attribute to the initial findings comes from press reports (The Block, Security Affairs, crypto.news) that agree with each other, not from Binance's own text. What the internal AI tool is, what it could do on other machines, whose container deployment token was taken, and what the script in the container did are not public. Teng's quote about approval controls reaches us through Elliptic, not a Binance document. Loss figures differ between Binance, TRM and Elliptic ($6.3 to $6.43 billion) because of pricing and counting choices. We could not read the FBI alert, Hacken's analysis or WIRED's reporting directly and cite none of them for detail. The delegation check ran against live chain data, but it only sees a delegation after it lands. The Kubernetes rule is a pySigma-parsed snippet, run only against synthetic audit events, and it only applies if your signing path runs on Kubernetes; Binance has not said that its does. The laptop hunting leads in section 4 are untested. We will update this article again when Binance publishes its full report.
